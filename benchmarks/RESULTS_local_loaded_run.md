## 4. Curve dedup payoff

n_events = 100,000. The per-curve build loop in `build_spline_table` (one `scipy.interpolate.CubicSpline` fit per curve, in a Python `for` loop -- see splines.py's builder) makes the true 20-syst x per-event (N=100,000 curves/syst) case far too slow to build within this benchmark's time budget (~25 minutes extrapolated, see below); measured directly at 1 systematic instead and linearly extrapolated to 20 (each systematic is an independent loop iteration in both the builder and `SplineTable._eval_all_curves`'s `vmap`).

|---|---|---|---|---|
| dedup, 1 syst (measured) | 200 | 141.74 ms | 0.4583 | 0.419 |

| no-dedup, 20 syst (EXTRAPOLATED x20) | 100000 | 1482.9 s | 39.638 | 200.0 |

**The dedup payoff is a *build-time* and memory payoff, with an eval-time penalty (no-dedup/dedup eval ratio 4.3x at 1 systematic, 9.7x at 20 systematics)**: at 1 systematic, no-dedup build is 523x slower than dedup build (74.1 s vs 142 ms) and its table+index uses 24x the memory, while no-dedup *eval* is 4.32x the dedup eval (1.982 ms vs 0.458 ms) for 500x more curves -- i.e. `SplineTable.__call__`'s vmapped Horner pass scales strongly sub-linearly in curve count on this CPU (the per-event gather dominates, not the per-curve Horner evaluation). The eval ratio is the least stable number in this benchmark (see 'Replicates and measurement noise' at the end: 1.2x-4.3x across three runs).
 At the full 20-systematic scale the measured dedup eval (4.065 ms) vs the extrapolated no-dedup eval (39.638 ms) gives ~9.7x (for scale: the whole fused dedup forward pass at this n_events is 10.6 ms, item 1), far from the 500x a naive O(n_curves) reading of `_eval_all_curves` would predict. The no-dedup eval numbers are 1-syst measurements scaled x20 (so this ratio compares a measurement with an extrapolation and can fall below 1 within noise), and would need re-checking at much larger curve counts or on a GPU. Absolute timings, especially the single-threaded Python build time, depend on machine load.

## 5. Sampler throughput (NUTS vs RWM)

n_events = 100,000, n_free = 15 (6 osc + 5 spline systs + 4 norm systs) throughout, except where noted.

**Headline finding: NUTS warmup (`blackjax.window_adaptation`) is the dominant cost, not the per-step gradient.** A full run of `bananafit.run_mcmc(algorithm='nuts', n_warmup=80)` on an n_events=20,000 instance of this same model measured **467.8 s for 80 warmup steps (5.85 s/step)** -- prior full run of this script (see RESULTS.md). Linearly extrapolating that to n_events=100,000 (scaling with item 1's measured grad-cost-vs-n_events) gives ~39 minutes for just 80 warmup steps -- i.e. this benchmark's entire ~25-minute time budget would be consumed several times over by NUTS warmup alone at the requested 1e5-event scale with default settings. This is the main scaling-cliff finding of this benchmark; see 'reading the numbers' and recommendations below for the likely cause (deep/unbounded trajectory trees from poorly-conditioned parameters -- e.g. `dm31`, `dm21` and `theta13` sit on very different natural scales in this synthetic model -- before `window_adaptation`'s mass-matrix estimate has converged).

Because of that finding, the throughput numbers below are a **bounded, non-adapted NUTS probe**: a fixed step size (0.05), identity mass matrix, and `max_num_doublings=6` (caps one trajectory at 64 leapfrog steps) at a reduced n_events=5,000 -- this deliberately avoids both the adaptation cost above and the risk of an unbounded trajectory, so it measures raw per-leapfrog-step throughput and the *shape* of the leapfrog-count distribution, not a properly tuned sampler's real performance (its ESS/s in particular should not be compared to a tuned run).

RWM proposal covariance from `bananafit.map_fit` (150 L-BFGS steps, 33.7 s, at n_events=100,000) + `bananafit.laplace_covariance` (44.1 s -- dominated by one-time Hessian-graph XLA compilation, not by n_events, see reading-the-numbers section), scaled by the Roberts-Rosenthal 2.38^2/d constant, run for 500 draws via a hand-rolled `additive_step_random_walk` scan at the full n_events=100,000. ESS via `bananafit.diagnostics.ess` (blackjax `effective_sample_size`) on that single short chain -- crude, not a converged-chain estimate.

## Reading the numbers

**Where the time goes.** At n_events=1,000,000, the dominant piece of the forward pass is **spline_product** (43.6306 ms of 85.7147 ms summed). 
DESIGN.md expects the oscillation (37.1040 ms) to dominate over the spline product (43.6306 ms, expected to be memory-bandwidth-ish rather than compute-bound) -- this measurement does NOT clearly show that ordering; see the per-scale component table above before treating this as a real deviation (component sizes are close and this machine is shared/noisy). 
The grad/forward ratio in item 1 (Poisson rows: 2.9-4.5x, median 3.9x; the Barlow-Beeston rows scatter more, partly CPU-load noise on a shared machine) is the expected few-x tax of reverse-mode AD through a fused elementwise+segment_sum+oscillation graph and is comparable to jaxnu's own documented ~3x forward/gradient ratio for the oscillation kernel alone, so the extra bananafit machinery (splines, norms, histogramming, priors) does not appear to add a disproportionate gradient tax.

**Practical CPU event-count ceiling.** At the largest measured scale (n_events=1,000,000), a single `logdensity`+`grad` pair costs 570.6 ms. A NUTS step needing O(10-100) gradient evaluations (item 5's measured leapfrog range was [7, 63]) therefore costs seconds at this scale once warmup is well-tuned, and tens of seconds when warmup is short/mistuned (as it was here, deliberately, for time budget -- see item 5's deviation note). Practically: **RWM scales comfortably to 1e6 events on this CPU, at a cost of one forward pass per step** (item 1's fwd column: 148 ms at n_events=1,000,000, 10.6 ms at 100,000; i.e. well under 10 steps/s at 1e6 events -- usable, not fast); **NUTS is comfortable up to roughly 1e4-1e5 events for exploratory/short chains**, but a full production NUTS fit (thousands of warmup + draws, well-tuned so leapfrog counts are smaller than the under-tuned ones seen here) at 1e5-1e6 events should be budgeted in CPU-hours, not minutes, unless the event count is reduced (e.g. pre-binned/pre-summed MC where physically valid) or warmup is tuned more aggressively for a shorter trajectory length.

**Expected GPU story (qualitative, not measured here -- CPU-only per the task).** Every hot-path op (oscillation nufast, spline Horner, norm product, segment_sum, Poisson/BB-lite reduction) is a dense, branch-free, embarrassingly-parallel elementwise or reduction op over the event axis -- exactly the shape that benefits most from a GPU: expect a substantial forward/grad speedup at 1e5-1e6 events (memory-bandwidth-bound pieces like the spline product should benefit less than compute-bound pieces like oscillation, all else equal). `jax.ops.segment_sum` is the one op worth watching -- its GPU implementation is a scatter-add, which is typically fine at these bin counts (tens to hundreds of bins) but would need checking if bin counts grew by orders of magnitude. Chain-parallelism (item 6) is the other place GPU should change the story qualitatively: on this CPU, more vmapped chains did not show clean amortization (see item 6's table -- total steps/s rises only 137 -> 151 (1.1x) for 8x more chains while steps/s/chain falls 137 -> 19, i.e. wall time grows nearly in proportion to the chain count (a modest throughput gain at best), since a single CPU core/thread pool has no spare parallel capacity to hide chain-batch latency behind); a GPU has the reverse profile (latency-bound at 1 chain, throughput-bound and heavily amortized at 4-64+ chains), so multi-chain NUTS/RWM is where a GPU is expected to pay off most, not single-chain per-step latency.

### Recommendations (no code changes made -- see task scope)

- **(headline) `run_mcmc(algorithm='nuts')`'s default `blackjax.window_adaptation` is a genuine scaling cliff, not just slow.** Measured directly: 467.8 s for 80 warmup steps at n_events=20,000 (5.85 s/step) -- extrapolated to ~39 minutes at n_events=100,000 for the same 80 steps (a real fit needs hundreds to a few thousand). The observed leapfrog-step distribution during (under-tuned, short) adaptation had a long tail toward `max_num_doublings`'s default cap (2**10 = 1024 leapfrog steps per transition) -- consistent with poorly-conditioned parameters confusing the diagonal-mass-matrix estimate before it converges (this synthetic benchmark model puts `dm21`, `dm31`, and angle parameters on very different natural scales with no informative priors to anchor them; a real DUNE-like analysis would normally add a reactor `theta13` constraint and physically-motivated priors that narrow the initial search, which should shrink this substantially). Recommend: (a) [done since this benchmark was first written: `run_mcmc` now exposes `max_num_doublings` and `target_acceptance_rate`, and `laplace_seeded_nuts` avoids window adaptation altogether; this benchmark's bounded probe still builds `blackjax.nuts` directly, as it predates that] (b) consider defaulting to a lower `max_num_doublings` (e.g. 8-10 is blackjax's default 10, but 6-8 bounds worst-case cost substantially for a modest exploration loss) for large-n_events / many-parameter models; (c) document in DESIGN.md/README that informative priors on scale-mismatched parameters (e.g. `dm21`/`dm31` vs mixing angles) are not just good statistical practice here but a practical warmup-cost mitigation.
- **Curve dedup pays off mainly at build time and in memory; the eval-time difference is small and noisy, at least up to the N=100,000-curve scale measured here.** Item 4's measured numbers: no-dedup build is ~523x slower than dedup build (a Python-level `scipy.interpolate.CubicSpline` call per curve, so build time is O(n_syst * n_curves), not O(n_syst) as the dedup case effectively is at fixed n_curves) and uses ~24x the memory at 1 systematic (scales the same at 20), while the jitted `SplineTable.__call__` eval is only ~4.3x the dedup one for 100,000 curves vs 200 in this run (1.2x-4.3x across the replicate runs; XLA vectorizes the per-curve Horner pass well). Practical implication: a caller who accidentally builds per-event splines will pay it back mainly as a slow, memory-hungry *build* step (item 4: 74 s and 10 MB for 1 systematic x 100,000 events vs 142 ms and 0.4 MB deduped) and at most a modest per-step eval penalty -- still worth avoiding (DESIGN.md Section 4 already assumes bin/dedup-before-`build_spline_table`), and a build-time warning if `n_curves` is a large fraction of `N` would be cheap insurance. The eval-time penalty is much smaller than a naive reading of `_eval_all_curves`'s O(n_curves) complexity would suggest, but that would need re-checking at curve counts well beyond 100,000, or on a GPU.
- **`build_spline_table`'s per-curve Python loop is the only build-time hot spot found.** At the dedup scale the package actually recommends (~hundreds of curves per systematic) it is fast (~1 s for 20 systs x 200 curves, item 1's build_s column), so this is not urgent, but if analyses grow toward thousands of curves per systematic (finer kinematic binning) it would be worth vectorizing (batched `CubicSpline` construction, or a hand-rolled tridiagonal solve over all curves of a systematic at once) rather than the current per-curve scipy call.
- **`laplace_covariance`'s Hessian graph is compile-cost-dominated, not size-dominated** (item 5: tens of seconds regardless of n_events, since the graph shape is the same for any N). That is a one-time, once-per-fit cost so not urgent, but it is worth knowing before assuming a slow `laplace_covariance` call means something is wrong with a particular dataset -- it is essentially a fixed tax for using it at all.
- **Prefer RWM (or a well-tuned/short-trajectory NUTS) as the default recommendation for large-n_events production fits today**, until the warmup-cost mitigations above land -- item 5/6 show RWM throughput is CPU-comfortable at 1e5 events (103 steps/s single-chain), although item 6 shows that vmapping RWM chains on this CPU raises total throughput only modestly (per-chain throughput falls nearly ~1/n_chains, so multi-chain runs cost wall time roughly proportional to the chain count on CPU), whereas plain NUTS's warmup cost at this scale is the dominant risk to a fit's wall-clock budget.
